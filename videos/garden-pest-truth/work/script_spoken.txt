Companion planting itself is genuinely old. Farmers have planted certain crops side by side for generations, long before anyone ran a controlled trial on why it seemed to work. Old-timers didn't need a study. They needed a garden that survived the season, and they passed down whatever actually did that, year after year, by observation alone.

The oldest and best-documented example is what's called the Three Sisters. Corn, beans, and squash, grown together. Corn gives beans something to climb. Beans pull nitrogen into the soil. Squash spreads low and shades out weeds. Indigenous farmers in North America were doing this centuries before modern agriculture had a name for it.

The version most people know today, the long list of specific pairings like "carrots love tomatoes," comes from a much more recent source. A nineteen seventy-five book that compiled garden folklore into charts. Genuinely useful reading for garden history. Not a research review, and the book itself contains no scientific citations.

That distinction mostly got lost over the decades. Both kinds of advice, the load-bearing agricultural science and the folklore compilation, ended up printed on identical-looking charts, with no way to tell which pairing was proven and which one was just a nice story.

Here's a useful tell. Agricultural scientists don't actually use the term "companion planting." They call it intercropping, or polyculture, and it's been studied in controlled field trials since the nineteen seventies. When you see that language, you're closer to the real research.

Garden magazines and seed catalogs picked up the folklore charts decades ago because they were simple, colorful, and easy to print. Nobody along that chain was checking the claims against an actual study. It just kept getting copied forward, generation after generation of gardeners, until it looked like settled wisdom instead of an unverified list.

So the old way isn't wrong to trust. Some of it is genuinely one of the oldest tested farming methods there is. We just have to be honest about which specific pairings actually belong in that category.

Let's go through exactly what's real, starting with the strongest evidence, and then exactly what to skip.

Real research on this breaks down into a short list of mechanisms that actually hold up. Trap cropping. Beneficial-insect support. Niche-complementary polyculture. And a few specific, provable chemical effects. Everything else is closer to folklore.

Start with the Three Sisters. Measured trials show a Land Equivalent Ratio of one point two to one point four for that combination. In plain terms, that means twenty to forty percent more total yield from the same patch of ground, compared to growing corn, beans, and squash separately. That's not a folk claim. That's a measured, replicated result.

Here's why it actually works, and it's worth understanding, not just memorizing. Corn and squash both draw heavily on nitrogen in the soil. Beans pull nitrogen out of the air and fix it into the ground through bacteria living on their roots. Planted together, the beans are quietly feeding the other two the entire season. That's a real, measurable chemical process, not a folk pairing.

Next, trap cropping, which is a real and different idea from most companion planting advice. You plant something pests prefer even more than your main crop, positioned to draw them away from it. Blue Hubbard squash planted near your main squash bed measurably pulls squash bugs and vine borers off the plants you actually want to protect, cutting damage thirty to sixty percent in replicated field trials.

The catch with trap cropping, and it's an important one, is that it takes active management. You have to actually monitor the trap crop and deal with the pests gathering on it. Skip that step, and you've just grown a healthier population of the exact bug you were trying to avoid, right next to your garden.

One more detail from the research worth knowing. A single trap crop planted alone is weaker than a mix of several. Trials in Finland used Chinese cabbage, marigolds, rapeseed, and sunflower planted together as a diverse trap crop, and it managed a specific beetle pest better than any one of those plants could on its own. Diversity, even in the decoy planting, measurably helps.

Third, and this one genuinely surprised researchers: sweet alyssum. A small flowering border plant that doesn't repel anything directly. Instead, it attracts the insects that eat your pests for you.

A University of California study planted sweet alyssum along lettuce fields specifically to test this. Researchers found it attracted enough parasitic wasps, green lacewings, and hoverflies that aphid populations dropped substantially, without spraying anything at all. One ladybug larva alone eats two to three hundred aphids as it grows, and a single planted border can pull those predators in from fields nearly fifty feet away.

Later trials measured that effect directly. Fifty to seventy percent lower aphid density. Two to four times more beneficial insects showing up. And that's with alyssum making up as little as ten percent of the planted bed area, a small border, not a major commitment of space.

Here's the actual mechanism, and it's a genuinely clever one. Predator insects like lacewings and parasitic wasps need two different things to survive. Protein, which they get from eating your pests. And carbohydrates, from nectar, to fuel them while they're out hunting. Sweet alyssum's tiny flowers are an easy, constant nectar source, so it keeps those predators living and breeding right next to your vegetables instead of passing through once and leaving.

Fourth: marigolds, but specifically for one job. French marigolds produce a compound called alpha-terthienyl in their roots, which is genuinely toxic to root-knot nematodes, microscopic worms that damage plant roots underground. Measured trials show a forty to sixty percent reduction in nematode populations, but only after eight to twelve weeks of the marigolds actively growing in that soil first.

Here's the part that matters, and where most companion planting charts overreach. That same marigold effect does not reliably carry over to pests that live above ground, on the leaves, like aphids or spider mites. The underground chemistry doesn't travel through the air the same way. Some controlled trials do show a real, measurable drop in certain pests like whiteflies when marigolds are planted early alongside a crop, through a different compound entirely. But other broad field trials on above-ground pests found no significant difference at all. The honest answer is that marigolds for above-ground pests are genuinely mixed evidence, not a clean yes.

Now the debunked side, starting with the famous one. "Carrots love tomatoes" has never held up under actual testing. It's folklore, not agricultural science, and planting them together does nothing measurable for either crop's pest resistance.

Mint gets recommended constantly as a pest deterrent. University trials specifically looking for that chemical deterrent effect found none. What mint actually does is out-compete nearby plants for space and water, which people mistake for pest control.

Onions and garlic supposedly keeping rodents away is another one that sounds plausible and has never actually been demonstrated in a controlled trial. Sometimes claimed, never shown.

Even the claim that basil improves a nearby tomato's flavor, which isn't about pests but shows up on every same chart, has no controlled trial behind it either. No measured flavor difference has ever been demonstrated.

Sunflowers suppressing weeds around them through their own natural chemicals is another one worth a second look. It genuinely happens in sterile pot trials in a lab. In actual garden soil, with real soil biology going on, that same effect mostly disappears. What works in a controlled pot doesn't always survive contact with a real backyard.

One more, and this one's a warning rather than a myth. Black walnut trees release a compound called juglone that genuinely poisons nearby plants. Tomatoes and peppers planted within sixty to eighty feet of a mature black walnut can be damaged or killed by it. If you've got one nearby, that's worth knowing before you plant anything sensitive close to it.

Let's cover what you're probably wondering.

I've got a small garden. Is any of this worth doing at that scale?

Yes, sweet alyssum and marigolds especially. Both work as border plants and don't need much space, and the beneficial-insect effect from alyssum has been measured working from a fairly small planted area.

Does using these methods mean I can skip pesticides entirely?

Not necessarily, especially with a heavy existing infestation. These are genuinely effective preventive and supportive tools, not guaranteed replacements for every situation. Think of them as reducing how often you need anything stronger, not eliminating the need completely.

How long before I actually see results?

Marigolds against nematodes need eight to twelve weeks of active growth before the effect shows up. Sweet alyssum's beneficial-insect effect builds over a season as the flowers establish and predator populations grow. None of this is instant.

Won't a trap crop just give pests a bigger population near my garden?

That's the real risk if you plant one and ignore it. A trap crop needs active management, meaning you check it and remove or treat the pests gathering there. Planted and forgotten, it can genuinely make things worse.

Does the type of marigold matter?

Yes. French marigolds specifically are the variety with measured nematode-suppressing roots. Other marigold types are mostly ornamental and haven't shown the same effect in trials.

I've been using a companion planting chart for years. Should I throw it out?

Not entirely. Keep what's on this list, and treat anything else on it as unproven rather than false. Some of it may still turn out to help through mechanisms nobody's tested yet. The honest position is "not yet shown," not "definitely useless."

Where do I actually get sweet alyssum or the right marigolds?

Both are inexpensive and common at almost any garden center or nursery, usually sold as seedlings in spring alongside other bedding flowers. Look specifically for "French marigold" on the tag.

Does any of this apply to houseplants, or is it strictly outdoor garden advice?

This is specifically outdoor research, done in real fields and garden beds. Indoor houseplant pests are a different situation entirely, without the same insect populations around to attract or manage, so don't expect the same results moving any of this indoors.

Will planting sweet alyssum and marigolds attract bees too?

Yes, genuinely, and that's a good thing for the rest of your garden. Both are popular with pollinators as well as the predator insects we're actually after, so you get a second benefit for the same planting.

Do I need to replant these every single year?

Sweet alyssum is typically grown as an annual and replanted each season, though it self-seeds readily in a lot of climates. French marigolds are also annuals. Neither is a one-time planting you can forget about permanently.

Can I actually combine several of these at once, or should I just pick one?

Combine them. Nothing about trap cropping, sweet alyssum, marigolds for nematodes, and the Three Sisters conflicts with any of the others. A well-planned garden using all four at once is genuinely stronger than leaning on just one.

Should I just buy ladybugs directly instead of attracting them with plants?

Store-bought ladybugs often fly off within a day or two if there's nothing keeping them around, since they're released, not established. Planting something like sweet alyssum gives resident populations an actual reason to stay and breed there, which works better over time than a one-time release.
