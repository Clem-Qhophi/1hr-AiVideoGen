So what is actually inside that number?
It is not one fee. It is three buckets of fees that happen to land on the same day.
Once you can see the three, the whole thing stops being mysterious.
Bucket one is what the bank charges you.
A fee for creating the loan. That is called origination, which means the bank's charge for setting your mortgage up.
A fee for checking you can repay it. That is underwriting, which is basically the bank doing its homework on you.
And the appraisal, where a licensed stranger confirms the house is really worth what you agreed to pay.
Here is the useful part. These fees change from lender to lender.
Two banks can offer the exact same interest rate and still charge hundreds of dollars apart in this bucket.
That is why getting more than one quote pays.
Bucket two is fees that are not really your bank's at all.
Title insurance, which is protection in case someone shows up later claiming they own part of your house. An old unpaid debt. A relative nobody mentioned.
A fee to your county for filing the paperwork that makes you the legal owner.
And in most states, a transfer tax. That is a fee your state or local government charges purely for changing whose name is on the property.
That last one is the whole story of this video.
Bucket three is money that is actually still yours.
Lenders usually make you pre-pay a year of home insurance. They also collect a few months of property tax up front and hold it in escrow, which just means the lender holds your money and pays those bills later.
It feels like a fee. But nobody keeps it. It is your own future bills, collected early.
Now back to that transfer tax. Because it is the one that swings.
Think of it as sales tax. But for a house.
Your bank does not set it. Your bank does not keep it. A state legislature set it, and your bank just collects it.
And it is charged as a percentage of the price. Hold onto that. It explains everything that comes next.
