The FHA will back a loan at three and a half percent down, per the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. On a three hundred thousand dollar house, that is about ten thousand five hundred dollars. Three completely different price tags to walk in the door. Path two. House hacking. Here is the trick almost nobody explains. Those same low-down-payment loans do not only work on a single house. They also work on a small building, two to four units, as long as you live in one unit yourself. So you buy the building with a beginner's down payment. You live in one unit. Your tenants' rent covers a chunk of your mortgage, sometimes all of it. Say the mortgage runs a bit over a thousand dollars a month, just as an example. If the other two units each rent for six hundred, that is twelve hundred dollars landing before you have paid a cent of your own. You are not just buying a home anymore. You are buying your first rental, and you are living inside it. Path three. Seller financing. This one skips the bank completely. The seller becomes the lender. You pay them directly, on terms you both agree to. Per Freedom Mortgage, a seller can accept a small down payment, or none at all, if they agree to it. There is no fixed rule. It is negotiated, deal by deal. Why would a seller ever say yes? Sometimes the house is hard to sell through a bank buyer. Sometimes they just want steady monthly income instead of one lump sum. Either way, it is their call. But the seller sets the terms, not a bank. That is worth remembering. We will come back to that. Path four. Wholesaling. This is the one that does not even require you to buy a house. You put a property under contract at an agreed price. Then, before you ever close, you sell that contract to another buyer for a fee. You never own the house. You never get a mortgage. You never make a down payment. The fee is just the gap. You lock in a price with the owner. You find someone willing to pay more for that same contract. You keep the difference. Per a survey of over a thousand wholesalers cited by RealEstateSkills, the average fee runs around thirteen thousand dollars a deal. Roughly the price of a decent used car, for one signed paper. Because two of those five paths deserve a serious warning. Wholesaling's legal rules are not the same everywhere. Some states barely regulate it. Others do, hard. Per RealEstateSkills and ResiMpli, South Carolina requires a license just to publicly market a contracted property. Pennsylvania's own law now treats it as licensed brokerage. Illinois caps an unlicensed wholesaler at one deal a year. So "wholesaling is legal" is true and false at the same time. It depends entirely on your state. Check your own state's rule before you try it, not after. Second warning. This is the catch I promised you, and it applies to every no-money-down path, not just wholesaling. Putting less down means you are borrowing more. Say a home drops five percent in value the year after you buy it, with zero down. Your loan does not shrink along with it. You can suddenly owe more than the house is worth, faster than a buyer who put real money down. Less skin in the game cuts both ways. It lowers your starting cost.
