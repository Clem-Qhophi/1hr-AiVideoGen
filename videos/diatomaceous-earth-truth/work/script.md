## method

[beat: walt explaining direct camera]
So let's go through exactly how this works, and exactly how to use it without getting hurt.

[beat: EVIDENCE-npic-mechanism]
Pesticide safety documentation is specific about the mechanism, and it's genuinely not what most people assume. This powder doesn't poison an insect. It kills by drying it out.

[beat: EVIDENCE-npic-mechanism]
Under a microscope, diatomaceous earth is made of countless tiny, sharp-edged particles. When an insect walks through it, those sharp edges scratch through the waxy outer coating on its exoskeleton, the layer that normally holds moisture in. Once that coating is damaged, the insect starts losing water it can't replace.

[beat: dehydrated insect illustration]
Without that protective layer, the insect dries out and dies, typically within a day or two. No nerve toxin, nothing absorbed into its body. It's closer to what would happen to you without skin, in a very dry room.

[beat: walt direct camera serious]
That's genuinely the whole mechanism. Physical damage to a waterproof coating, followed by dehydration. It's also exactly why this stuff never expires, and why insects can't evolve resistance to it. You can't evolve armor against being cut and dried out.

[beat: chemical pesticide molecule]
Compare that to a chemical spray, where the active ingredient has to bind to a specific receptor in the insect's nervous system to work. Change that receptor slightly, through random mutation and enough generations, and the poison stops binding. That's genuinely how pesticide resistance happens in the real world, and it's a real, growing problem for pest control chemicals. There's no equivalent shortcut around a sharp edge and dry air.

[beat: EVIDENCE-npic-mechanism]
Pesticide regulators list it as effective against bed bugs, cockroaches, crickets, fleas, ticks, spiders, ants, and a long list of other crawling insects. Not flying insects in the air, since it only works on contact. But nearly anything that walks across it.

[beat: food-grade bag closeup]
Now, the part that actually matters for your safety. Diatomaceous earth sold for pest control comes in two genuinely different forms, and they are not interchangeable.

[beat: EVIDENCE-pestzero-grades]
Food-grade diatomaceous earth is simply crushed and milled, no extreme heat involved. It's mostly a soft, porous form of silica, and it's considered safe enough that it's used as a food additive and in some health supplements.

[beat: pool filter grade warning]
Pool-grade, sometimes called filter-grade, goes through a completely different process. It's heated past a thousand degrees Celsius in a process called calcining, which changes the structure of the silica itself, converting most of it into something called crystalline silica.

[beat: EVIDENCE-pestzero-grades]
That distinction is not cosmetic. Food-grade diatomaceous earth contains less than one percent crystalline silica. Pool-grade can contain up to seventy-five percent. Breathing that in repeatedly is a genuine, documented cause of a lung disease called silicosis, and crystalline silica is classified as a known human carcinogen.

[beat: applying powder baseboards]

## objections

[beat: question mark simple graphic]
Let's cover what you're probably wondering.

[beat: pool store diatomaceous earth]
I've got a bag of pool-grade diatomaceous earth already. Can I just use that instead?

No, and this is the one to actually remember. Pool-grade is processed specifically for water filtration, not for scattering around your home, and the crystalline silica content makes repeated exposure a real respiratory risk. Keep it in the pool shed, not your kitchen.

[beat: pets safe diatomaceous earth]
Is it actually safe around pets?

Food-grade diatomaceous earth is commonly used in pet care, including some pet supplement products, and it's considered safe once any airborne dust has settled. Still avoid letting a pet inhale a fresh application directly, same as you would.

[beat: how long working powder]
How long does it actually take to work?

Most insects die within one to two days of real contact, since dehydration isn't instant. It's not a spray that drops a bug on contact. Think of it as a trap that works over time, not an instant kill.

[beat: garden vegetable safe use]
Can I use it in my vegetable garden?

Yes, food-grade diatomaceous earth is commonly used around garden plants for crawling pests, and it's approved for that use. Just reapply after watering or rain, since moisture is what stops it from working.

[beat: eating swallowing safety]
What happens if a kid or pet actually eats some?

Food-grade diatomaceous earth is not considered poisonous, and very little of it is absorbed if swallowed. It's not something to encourage, but an accidental taste of the food-grade kind is not a poisoning emergency the way a chemical pesticide would be.

[beat: bed bugs mattress use]
Does it actually work on bed bugs?

Yes, it's specifically registered for bed bug control, and it's a genuinely popular option because bed bugs can't develop resistance to it the way they increasingly have to some chemical treatments. Dust it lightly along mattress seams, bed frames, and baseboards.

[beat: how often reapply]
How often do I need to reapply it?

Indoors, in a dry area, a single application can keep working for weeks or months undisturbed. Outdoors, or anywhere it gets wet or heavily trafficked, plan on reapplying every week or two.

[beat: does it stain damage]

Food-grade diatomaceous earth is a fine, chalky white powder, and it can leave visible residue on dark surfaces until it's vacuumed up. It doesn't chemically damage most household surfaces, but a light dusting is easier to clean up than a thick one.

[beat: ant hill outdoor lawn]

Yes, dusting it directly into the entrance of a mound can work, though outdoor use is genuinely harder to keep dry. A colony that's already deep and established may take repeat applications before you see it thin out.

[beat: exterminator cost comparison]

Substantially, for most everyday pest problems. A single service call from a professional exterminator commonly runs into the hundreds of dollars. A bag of food-grade diatomaceous earth and a bulb duster together usually cost less than a fast food order, and can cover an entire home.
